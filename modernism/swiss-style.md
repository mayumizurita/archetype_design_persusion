# Swiss Style (International Typographic Style)
[Back to modernist design](README.md) · [Home](../README.md)

## What is it?

Swiss Style, also called the International Typographic Style, developed in Switzerland in the postwar decades, especially through the Zürich and Basel schools. It favored clear visual communication: asymmetrical layouts, typographic grids, sans-serif type, restrained color, and photography or simple graphic forms. The style was influential well beyond Switzerland, but its principles are tools rather than a guarantee that every message will be clear.

## How do you recognize or use it?

Look for consistent alignments, an obvious hierarchy, concise copy, generous negative space, and carefully limited visual decoration. A grid helps people scan a headline, supporting information, and a call to action in a predictable order. For persuasive design, this can make a complicated offer easier to compare and understand; the clarity can support trust, but it cannot make an unsupported claim true. Avoid using “minimal” as an excuse to omit useful context or accessible contrast.

## Examples

### 1. Josef Müller-Brockmann, *Musica Viva*, 1957

The [MoMA collection record](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/4885) identifies this concert poster by Swiss designer Josef Müller-Brockmann. Its sharply organized type and abstract geometric image show how a strong formal system can make a cultural event visually distinctive while keeping the information structured. In persuasive communication, this kind of hierarchy can help a reader find the event, message, and next step quickly. Image: MoMA collection image; linked for study, not reproduced here. Check MoMA/Scala permissions before reuse.

### 2. Armin Hofmann, *Giselle, Basler Freilichtspiele*, 1959

The [MoMA collection record](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/6775) identifies Hofmann’s 1959 photolithograph for Basler Freilichtspiele. Its dance image and stable type show how one photograph can add movement while the information remains ordered. That balance can draw attention to a persuasive message without making its action hard to locate. Image: MoMA collection image; linked for study, not reproduced here. Check MoMA/Scala permissions before reuse.
